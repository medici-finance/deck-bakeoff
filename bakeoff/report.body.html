1
What the bake–off was

One brief, two media, two brand modes, thirty–six artifact pairs, judged twice.

On 27 August 2026 a fleet of Claude Code agents was asked to settle an argument by building rather than by discussing it: which AI presentation–design skill, and which messaging method, should the house adopt as its default? The argument was settled the only way a tooling argument can be — by giving every candidate the same source material, the same audience, the same deadline and the same two deliverables, then having independent reviewers grade what came out.

The subject was a product pitch for Medici for RWA tokens: a collateral–splitting primitive for tokenized real–world assets. The pitch had to teach three things — what the product is in one repeatable sentence, why a tokenized–asset holder cares, and how the basic mechanism works, in one diagram the audience could redraw. Every contender read the same corpus and every contender was forbidden to invent a number.

The matrix

The nominal design space is 2 × 6 × 4 — two brand modes, six design tools, four messaging skills. It did not render as forty–eight, and the reason is worth stating plainly. The messaging leg ran first, because the design layer consumes its output. Three messaging contenders finished in time to feed stage two; the fourth — the house messaging method — was added afterwards, at Ian’s instruction, and was reviewed but never rendered. So the design layer is a complete 2 × 6 × 3, and the messaging layer is a complete 4 × 4 cross–review. Where this report says “track–mode” it means one design tool in one brand mode — twelve of them.

The two media

Deck
index.html — self–contained, a fixed 1280×720 stage, keyboard–navigable, printing one slide per page. Ten to fourteen slides, about ten minutes spoken. No remote assets except web fonts, and only in free mode.
Explainer
explainer.html plus a SCRIPT.md talk track — a self–contained animated film at 1920×1080, meant to be screen–recorded. Scenes advance on a timeline; each scene’s duration equals the narration budget in the script. Ninety to a hundred and fifty seconds. No text–to–speech, no mp4, no external media: Ian renders. Motion had to carry meaning — the split, the payoff at maturity — and decoration–only animation was out of bounds.

The contract between the two legs

The bake–off is not two independent competitions bolted together. Each messaging contender emitted a staging contract — a machine–readable file (a staging.yaml) that fixes what is said and in what order: one function and one headline claim per slot, one designated bold moment (the single beat the whole argument is built to arrive at), and, for film, a per–scene motion role and word budget. Design contenders consumed that contract verbatim: never reorder a slot, never rewrite a headline claim, and log any slot you cannot render rather than dropping it silently. That is what makes the twelve track–modes comparable at all — they are twelve renderings of the same argument, not twelve different arguments.

3
The messaging leg

All four methods told the same story. Only their emphasis and their order diverged — and every divergence traces to the method, not to taste.

3.1   What all four did in common

The convergence is the first finding, and it is not luck. The brief pre-specifies the one-liner, the value proposition and the mechanism as three named educational goals, and the source corpus is strongly opinionated. Given that, all four independently arrived at the same spine: one position → two claims → they always sum back → therefore no liquidation price exists; the same value proposition; and the same payoff table with the invariant as the thing to remember. All four reuse the same five proof points, the same five objections and the same terminology canon.

Common to all fourEvidence
Zero invented figures.Every numeric token in all deliverable files was run against the corpus. Every number that does not appear in the sources is staging metadata — energies, runtime shares, word budgets, per-slot seconds. Four independent fact-checks, one result.
Same spine, same proof points.Problem → the split → the invariant → the payoff table → the honest cost. Same five objections in reserve, same terminology canon.
Both corpus contradictions logged, none smoothed.The template-count conflict and the collateral-fit conflict are carried by every contender rather than resolved silently — though in three different places.
Single pass, ten to eighteen minutes.No contender needed to rework its argument. The slowest was the one that had no transport format and had to borrow another skill’s field names.
Each emitted a machine-checkable contract.Every contender produced a staging contract the design layer could parse — though only three of the four produced one that parses cleanly against the closed vocabulary.

3.2   Per-skill summary

Scores are the model judge’s, on three axes: fit (inverse difficulty — ten means the tool fitted the job with little adaptation), representation quality against the brief’s three educational goals, and contract fitness — how well its output survives being consumed by six design tracks.

staging FIT 9 · QUALITY 8 · CONTRACT 9 · ~10.5 MIN

The only contender whose skill was built for this job, and the only one whose native output is the transport contract. Closed-vocabulary functions, a claim and a beat on every slot, a bold moment whose placement is derived separately per medium rather than inherited across media, and video arithmetic that reconciles to the digit: 299 words written against a 300-word budget, scene durations summing to exactly 120 seconds, per-scene delivery between 144 and 156 words per minute. It ran seven machine checks on its own output and all seven hold on re-run. Three video gates it could not run from the messaging layer are recorded NOT RUN with the owner named rather than fudged to pass — the only contender that had to make that call.

Against it. Its own notes carry the sharpest self-criticism in the leg: the deck landed at fourteen slots partly because fourteen is the count at which the bold-moment window becomes satisfiable (twelve gives 41.7%, thirteen gives 38.5%, fourteen gives 42.9% against a 40–50% window) — the arithmetic led the argument for one pass. The skill has no notion of a corpus: no per-slot source field, no objections block, no terminology canon, no provenance-conflict block; all four had to be invented as extensions. And the one-diagram specification lives in its prose artifact, not in the contract the design layer is told to consume — a design contender reading only the contract gets no diagram guidance at all.

house-guide FIT 6 · QUALITY 9 · CONTRACT 8 · ~18 MIN

The highest editorial ceiling in the leg, and the only contender no rubric places worse than third. It is the only one that mandates a worked example — one deposit, one strike, three prices, three payouts — which is the gap every rubric marked against everyone else. Its number gate is the strongest single artifact anyone produced: a verdict per figure, as-of dates in visible text rather than footnotes, three explicit WITHHOLD rows, and the only contender to catch that a widely quoted market figure was an exchange’s summary of a research report rather than the report, and then decline it. It has the only real gloss table (seventeen rows) and the only rule refusing to name prospects.

Against it. The method has no transport format at all — no slot count, ordering, timing, bold moment, motion vocabulary or genre — so its entire contract was built by mapping prose sections onto another skill’s field names. Its own gates certify things its file contradicts: three statements disagree about when the first number arrives, and the glossary gate reports a pass over a slot shipping three unglossed terms against its own rule. Twelve of its thirteen claims exceed the twelve-word cap two rivals held, and its bold moment sits at 76.9% by index — behind the relief rather than in front of it.

3.4   What every reviewer agreed on

  1. No invented numbers, in any of the four. Every content figure traces to a named file and slide. Notably, the clean sheet was verified by hand: two of the four rubrics have no provenance machinery at all, and one says outright that a contender which invented a number would have passed all fifteen of its rows.
  2. The template-count conflict is unresolvable from the corpus. Three contenders take the higher figure on the brief’s source-priority rule and log the conflict; house-guide withholds it. All four notice the thing that matters — the later revision carries the lower number, so the priority rule may be selecting the stale figure.
  3. Unglossed jargon survives every method. One three-term phrase ships bare in three of four contenders; house-guide clears those and then ships three different bare terms on its cost slot. Every rubric flagged it; no contender’s own gate caught it — and the one contender with a real gloss table certified a pass over its own violation.
  4. The fee is missing from the transport in three of four. Only pitch-deck-mastery puts the transaction fee on a slot; house-guide marks it ship-worthy in its own number gate and then omits it. Otherwise six design tracks ship a pitch that never says what it costs. One rubric dissents and calls that slot a cut candidate — so the fee is a must-pass row on one rubric and second-meeting material on two.
  5. The legal entity is on the cover. Three of four put it there; the corpus itself records that naming that entity triggers an automatic sanctions review at US institutions. Spending it in the first ten seconds buys nothing. One contender spends it twice.
  6. The collateral-fit question is a positioning fork, not a fact-check. Four methods produced four different answers to the same question — one disqualifies the flagship collateral on a slide, one in the ask, one names the requirement and refuses to name an underlying, one keeps the brief’s framing. Nobody was wrong; nobody could close it.

Messaging verdict

Adopt staging as the transport and house-guide as the content gate — one pass, not a pick.

staging is still the only contender with a legal genre, a classification block carrying its rejections, one mechanism slot and a bold moment in front of the relief — and the contract is what six design tracks consume. But house-guide beats it decisively on the editorial layer, nine against eight, so its number gate (a verdict per figure, as-of dates in visible text, withholding as a legitimate outcome) and its gloss table become required blocks in the contract, and scene → value → mechanism → honest boundary becomes the default arc check.

4
The design leg

No tool scores above seven for fit, because all six had to build the timed–explainer engine themselves.

4.1   What all six did in common

The design leg’s most important finding is not about any one tool. Six skills, chosen from three different lineages, converged on the same set of things they all had to build from scratch — and each named the gap independently, in its own notes, before any reviewer read them.

Every tool had to…What that meant, in their own words
Build the film engine itself. None of the six has a timeline concept. “No motion vocabulary at all”; “could not express: a scene timeline, narration timing”; “covers interaction motion, not a fixed-duration film with a seek API”; “no scene/timeline concept at all”; “the skill has no timeline”. All six independently built the same surface: a frame clock, a per-scene duration attribute equal to the narration budget, a scene deep-link, a progress bar and a player object. This is the bake-off’s clearest tooling gap.
Borrow the motion vocabulary from the messaging layer. The eight motion verbs the films are built on came from the messaging contracts’ motion-role field, not from any design skill. Every tool says so; the messaging side says the same thing from the other direction.
Replace the transport. Each tool assumes a scrolling responsive page, or a viewport-fluid deck, or a single printed page. All six built the fixed letterboxed stage, the film stage, print pagination, keyboard navigation and a slide counter from the task brief rather than from the skill.
Ship one generator plus three content files. Every tool built a shared system once and then rendered the three messaging contracts through it. Rebuild times after the system existed: 2.1 s, 0.03 s, under 1 s. Per-artifact time is therefore not comparable to hand-authoring, and all six say so.
Render every slot, including the awkward one. One messaging contract carries an appendix slot outside its numbered list. All six rendered it rather than dropping it, and all six flagged the call. Contract fidelity is perfect across every artifact measured: 30 of 30 decks and 30 of 30 explainers — the ten track-modes the fidelity rubric covered, the finish arm inheriting its baseline — render every slot and scene, in order, claims verbatim, footer on every surface, durations equal to the narration budget.
Fight the same linter false positive. The brand linter’s leak rule contains a substring of every real citation in the corpus, so honest sourcing hard-fails it. Five of six notes files report it as top-tier friction.
Invent a way to photograph a moving film. Headless rendering advances the script clock but not the animation clock, so every mid-animation screenshot comes back at time zero — which reads exactly like a broken build. Every tool that rendered frames hit this independently and each invented its own freeze hook. One notes file says the bug “would have silently shipped an unverified film”.
Discover that looking is what finds the defects. Every contender that looked at frames says looking is what found the real bugs; every contender that could not look names that as its top risk. The finish contender puts it most sharply: a detector result is defect evidence, not proof of quality.

4.2   Per-tool summary

Two frames per tool per brand mode, all from the same messaging lane so they are comparable: the mechanism slide — the one slide the brief says a viewer must be able to redraw — and the bold-moment scene, the film’s designated peak. Scores are the model judge’s: fit, then representation quality.

canvas-design BRAND 3 / 9  ·  FREE 3 / 7  ·  DELTA −1.5

The worst fit and the best result. The skill’s model is one page — it states “output only .md, .pdf, .png” three times — with no sequence, no motion vocabulary, no HTML, and an ethic (never explain, let the composition tell the story) that inverts an educational brief. About 40% of the session went on building the structural requirements the skill does not have. What saved it is one instruction: systematic reference markers from an imaginary discipline turns out to be the same instruction as the brief’s every figure must trace to a source, so provenance became a design element rather than a compliance tax. The result is 42 of 42 exhibits and 42 of 42 file-level citations, with alt-text on every drawing.

Its mechanism slide is the one payoff diagram a viewer could redraw unaided: the two legs stacked, the constant edge held flat, the strike marked, and both branches written as arithmetic under the axis. Its bold-moment scene — fifty-odd hairline marks falling to a zero baseline under one enormous figure — is one of only two genuine visual peaks in the field, and a later slide draws a non-existence, which nothing else attempted. One reviewer calls that plate “the single best visual in the bake-off: it draws a non-existence”.

Against it. The least legible deck measured: 123 of 221 strings under the 4.5:1 contrast floor, with the source line running at 1.85:1. And it is the only track in thirty decks that dropped a fact. 8.1   The human rulings still needed

Four independent methods reached these six and none could close any of them. Each is a judgment about what is true or what the pitch should be, and each blocks something concrete: the first two sit on slide three of every deck in the field.

#The questionWhy it is a human’s and not a method’s
1The build-size figures. 54 or 36 contract templates — and the two matching line counts. The one question no contender could close, and one says so outright. Three took the higher figure on the brief’s source-priority rule; the fourth withheld it. All four noticed that the later source revision carries the lower number, so the priority rule may be selecting the stale figure. If the lower figure is right, every deck in this bake-off overstates the build on a first-contact slide — precisely the class of error a diligence process kills a conversation over. Ian states the number; the priority rule does not get to decide this one.
2The venue claim. “$344B represented / $2T a month in repo / the #1 RWA network”. Asserted by the product’s own deck with no external citation anywhere in the corpus, and sitting directly against a research document in the same corpus which repeatedly finds that this venue appears nowhere in either part of the 2026 lending map. All four contenders put it on a slide, two inside a headline claim; only one puts the counter-evidence on the same slide. It needs a source, or a hedge, before it leaves the room.
3Which underlying the pitch names. The brief’s product story leads with money-market and treasury collateral; the corpus’s own research says near-zero-volatility treasuries are the wrong first underlying and names tokenized gold instead. Four methods produced four different answers. It is a positioning ruling, not a fact-check — and it changes what the deck is, so it cannot be left to four contenders to decide differently.
