# Lineage

This skill is a **restatement**. Almost nothing in it is original; what is
original is the arrangement, the two fields nobody else carries (bold-moment
*placement* and time shape in *minutes*), and the boundary that keeps design
out of staging.

The rules we followed while writing it, and that anyone editing it must keep:

1. **Methods are restated in house words.** Under US copyright law a
   presentation methodology is a procedure or system and is not protected
   (17 U.S.C. §102(b)); the books, diagrams, worked examples and template
   files that describe those methods are. So: the shape of an arc, described
   as rules, is ours to write. The author's drawing of that arc is not, and
   neither is their prose.
2. **No source's trademarked or branded name is used as our operating
   vocabulary.** Not as a feature name, not as a mode, not as a field value.
   Several of the names below are asserted marks and at least one is
   registered. We refer to them by author and title here, in a
   further-reading note, and nowhere else in the skill.
3. **No verbatim prose, no reproduced figures, no vendored template files.**
   Where a source ships a labelled N-item list, we re-derived and re-ordered
   rather than paraphrasing down the list — selection-and-arrangement is
   protectable even where the individual rules are not.
4. **Numeric parameters are facts and are used as such**, with the source
   named. Where a number is a practitioner convention rather than a finding,
   the skill says so.

## What we took, and from whom

**Barbara Minto, *The Pyramid Principle*.** The situation/complication/
question/answer opening and — more usefully — the rule that its four elements
are *reordered according to audience state*. The receptivity gate:
answer-first for a receptive audience, argument-first for a hostile one. The
rule that a group's summary must state the insight rather than label the
group, which is where our "claim, not label" test comes from. Our house
vocabulary avoids her branded terms.

**Andrew Abela, the Extreme Presentation™ method.** The richest single source
here. Three things: (a) the two-idiom split — perform-to-a-crowd vs
persuade-at-a-table, with concrete density, medium and interaction parameters
— which is our hall/table routing in §CLASSIFY; (b) the four-beat middle whose
complication is re-raised as the audience's *next most likely objection*,
cycling until the objections run out, which is our objection loop; (c) the
transition rule that a page should end on the complication so the page-turn
lands on curiosity — our "end each section owing" rule. Also his framing that
objectives describe changes in the *audience*, never in the presenter, and
that an update with no think-or-do change should not be presented at all. His
method name and his arc's acronym are asserted marks and appear nowhere in
this skill; his Chart Chooser and Slide Chooser diagrams are all-rights-
reserved (the widely repeated belief that they are Creative Commons is false)
and are neither reproduced nor relied on — they are design artifacts and this
skill does not do design.

**Garr Reynolds, *Presentation Zen* / *The Naked Presenter*.** The opening
taxonomy — personal story, the unexpected, the never-before-seen, the
challenge to assumption, the relevant humorous anecdote — which we merged with
Weissman's gambit list into a single unbranded set rather than reproducing
either. The roughly-ten-minute attention interval and the deliberate gear
change (three to four minutes online), which is our attention-budget table.
The rules that you do not end on Q&A and that the artifact and the handout are
different documents, which is our presented/read fork. His anti-formula stance
is honoured in the SKILL's insistence that a stub playbook is unusable and
that beats are deleted rather than filled with invented material. Three widely
circulated attributions to him — jo-ha-kyu, kishotenketsu, and an "elevator
test" — were checked against primary sources and are **not his**; they do not
appear here. His name is a registered mark and is used nominatively only.

**Michael Alley, assertion–evidence.** The sentence-form claim as the slide's
headline with the evidence beneath it, and the arc-level consequence we made
into a gate: extract the headline list and read it alone; if it does not stand
as an argument, the arc is broken. This is our helicopter test. It is also the
only method in the corpus with a real published comprehension-and-retention
literature behind it, which is why the claim-per-slot rule is stated as
non-negotiable rather than as a preference.

**Jerry Weissman, *Presenting to Win*.** The idea that the *middle* has an
enumerable, finite set of shapes you select from rather than invent — our
genre playbooks are that idea applied at a coarser grain. His seven opening
gambits, merged with Reynolds' set as above. The audience-benefit rule
(stated from the audience's side, repeated, not delivered once) sits behind
our audience block's "decision they hold".

**Cliff Atkinson, *Beyond Bullet Points*.** The only arithmetic
time-distribution rule in the literature: the same content pruned to depth by
available minutes, so "give me the short version" is a mechanical cut rather
than a rewrite. Our `time_shape` and its floors are that idea, generalised
from slide counts to minutes. The script-before-slides discipline is the same
instinct as our composition contract. We built our own artifact shape rather
than shipping his template.

**Nancy Duarte, *Resonate*.** Two imports: the deliberately engineered
memorable peak (our bold moment — the *field* is hers in spirit, the
*placement rule* is what we added, and it is genre-specific here where hers is
general), and the audience-as-protagonist inversion, which is why our audience
block asks what decision they hold rather than what we want. Her drawn arc
figure is a copyrighted illustration and is neither reproduced nor imitated;
her branded terms are not used.

**Chip & Dan Heath, *Made to Stick*.** The knowledge curse as a pre-flight
diagnostic — you cannot un-know what you know, so you communicate at your own
level — which is the first failure mode named in `audience.md`. The
concrete-over-abstract rule and the observation that a credible ordinary
instance can outperform an authority both feed our "instance, not category"
rule for the explainer's bold moment.

**Alan Monroe's motivated sequence, and classical rhetorical arrangement.**
The unbranded skeleton — attention, need, satisfaction, visualisation, action;
and exordium/narratio/partitio/confirmatio/refutatio/peroratio. Both are
effectively common property (one published at Purdue in the 1930s and restated
in thousands of textbooks, the other Roman), which is why the arcs here rest on
that ground with the modern methods layered on as restated refinements. The
classical *refutatio* slot is the ancestor of our `objection` function.

**Robert McKee, on scene structure.** The value-change test — a scene must
turn something — restated as our dead-slot gate. It is the single most useful
lint rule we found, and it is the one that cuts agenda slides.

**Video-side sources for the video lanes.** Guo, Kim & Rubin (ACM Learning@
Scale, 2014, 6.9M sessions) for the sub-six-minute segmentation ceiling and
the finding that pre-production planning beats post-production polish — the
peer-reviewed anchor for the video lane. Derek Muller's misconception-first
sequencing result (surface the wrong model, allow the confusion, then
correct), which is why the technical genre's video lane makes it mandatory.
Grant Sanderson's published criteria — clarity, motivation established in the
opening half-minute, novelty, memorability, concrete before abstract. Dan
Harmon's story circle for the beat that commercial video reliably omits — the
cost or tradeoff — whose absence we treat as a trust tell in both media.
Kurzgesagt's published process for the discipline of rewriting until the
structure is right. The commercial explainer's converging runtime shares and
the words-per-minute arithmetic that makes video staging zero-sum come from
the trade template literature (Vidico, mypromovideos, Boords) and are
presented here as conventions with provenance, not as findings.

**Agentic prior art.** The idea of a genre → audience → arc encoding as
*data* rather than prose — playbooks with pick/skip conditions, ordered beats,
kill conditions and quality gates — is already in the wild: `appautomaton/
presentation` (MIT-declared), `oalanicolas/aiox-advanced-brain` (MIT),
`mohitagw15856/pm-claude-skills` (MIT), `RehgLab/ArcDeck` (MIT). We took
patterns, not files: the named transition types and the rising-stakes ladder
with an if-skipped column (appautomaton, renamed and re-worded here); the
structural-sag diagnosis — early sag, middle sag, late sag, each with a
distinct cause and the note that the fix is re-sequencing rather than more
energy in delivery (pm-claude-skills); closing a large relation inventory down
to a handful, each earning a distinct *sequencing consequence* (ArcDeck's
treatment of rhetorical structure theory), which is the move behind our closed
sixteen-function vocabulary; and per-genre YAML with pick/skip, ordered beats,
evidence policy and gates (aiox). From aiox we also took a **correction**: its
schema carries a `visual_family` field per slot, and that field is exactly the
leak our composition contract exists to prevent. We dropped it deliberately.
Two content-rich sources — `marcusnelson/presentation-writing-claude-skill`
and `nraford7/Narrative-Engine` — carry **no licence at all**; their schemas
informed our thinking and nothing was copied from them.

**HyperFrames motion doctrine (`heygen-com/hyperframes`, Apache-2.0).** The one
place in this skill where a file is **vendored rather than restated**:
`tools/seam-gate.mjs` is their script, copied under the Apache-2.0 licence with
the notice preserved, a copy of the licence beside it, and the two
modifications (a provenance header; the shebang moved to line 1) stated in that
header per §4(b). We took the file because it is a *measurement*, not a method
— a rule restated in house words is still a rule, but a verifier restated in
house words is a different verifier, and the value here is that this one has
been run against real films. What we did NOT take: their CLI, their project
format, their frame presets, and their prose. Two ideas from the surrounding
doctrine are restated rather than copied: that a scene boundary has a *vector*
which both sides must share (our declared-seam ledger), and that a crossfade
has no carrier and is therefore not a transition (our `crossfade: 0`
requirement, which their gate enforces numerically as zero-overlap). Their
reference doc never mentions running the gate outside a HyperFrames project;
`references/seam-gate.md` is our own write-up of the standalone wiring, which is
discoverable only by reading `ensureServer()`.

**`iart-ai/motion-design-skills` (MIT).** The **form** of
`references/motion-spec.md` — a one-choice-per-row motion-language table
covering easing family, base timing unit, transition family, stagger rhythm,
motion intensity and hold discipline, locked before authoring and audited after
— is theirs, and it is a genuinely good form: it forces the decisions that
otherwise get made forty times, differently, one scene at a time. What is ours:
the house numbers in every row, the intensity-budget row, and the change the
review made — **every distance is a percentage of frame height, never a pixel
count**, because `24px` is not a motion language, it is a number that happens to
be true of one output resolution. Their preset personalities and their tone-and-
energy grid informed our numbers and are not reproduced; where their presets
carry overshoot, the house rule overrides it to zero and the override is
recorded rather than silently applied. MIT-licensed, so the form could have been
copied; it was restated anyway, for consistency with rule 1 above.

**Slide-engine vocabulary.** Seven of our sixteen function names match
Slidev's layout vocabulary (MIT) so a staged artifact maps onto an existing
renderer without translation. Slidev supplies names, not sequencing rules;
the sequencing rules are ours.

## What is ours

- The hall/table lane as a *staging* input that sets density, appendix
  legality and the read/presented fork — the split is Abela's, its use as a
  routing gate at the top of the flow, ahead of genre, is our arrangement.
- **`bold_moment.placement`** as a genre-specific, gate-checked field. Every
  source that has the concept treats placement as taste. Making it a rule per
  genre — after the mechanism for a deep dive, before any relief for an
  explainer — with a stated failure mode for getting it wrong, is the piece
  none of the surveyed work carries.
- **`time_shape` in minutes with a per-slot floor**, for two media, and the
  requirement that the arithmetic be shown. The literature counts slides;
  slides are not a unit of time.
- The composition contract as a **testable** boundary in both directions, and
  the rule that a failed gate names an upstream step to return to rather than
  being patched where it failed.
- The requirement that CLASSIFY emit **stated rejections** — a classification
  with no rejections was not a decision.
- **The kinetic governor** (v2). The eight motion roles, the 0–5 energy
  allowance, and the six allocation rules are ours. Duarte has the peak; nobody
  in the surveyed corpus allocates *energy across an arc* as a staging field
  with a budget, and nobody derives the peak count mechanically. The derivation
  rule — `rupture` and `arrest` are legal only on a claim containing an EVENT,
  so you count the events and that is the peak budget — is the piece we trust
  most, because it is mechanical and unarguable, which is the profile of every
  rule here that has earned its place.
- **The ornament gate (R6)**, and the fact that it is a *scene-internal* budget
  rather than another intensity rule. It exists because the intensity budget
  was obeyed and decoration shipped anyway.
- **`bold_moment.placement` as a DERIVED per-medium window.** v1 made placement
  a rule where the corpus made it taste; v2 makes it an arithmetic consequence
  of the lane's own `runtime_shares`. The specific finding — that a
  deck-calibrated percentage was unsatisfiable by construction in a video lane,
  so the gate could not be passed by any staging of that genre — is what a
  procedure catching its own author looks like, and it is why the rule is now
  the derivation rather than the number.
- **The video GATE's render-and-LOOK requirement.** Every other gate in this
  skill is a reasoning check. This one insists a frame is actually seen,
  because the two defects that survived every automated check on the runs
  behind v2 — an illegible soft-wrap and a scene that finished its motion in
  the first sixth of its slot — are both invisible to measurement and obvious
  to an eye.
