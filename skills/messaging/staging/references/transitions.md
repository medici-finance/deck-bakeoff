# Transitions — five types and a stakes ladder

A transition is not a slide and not an animation. It is the **sentence between
two beats** that tells the audience what just changed. In a deck it is spoken
(and belongs in the speaker notes); in a video it is the line over the cut.

Staging assigns a transition **type** to every beat boundary. A boundary with
no assigned type is a boundary where the audience does not know why the
subject changed — which they experience as the artifact being disorganised,
even when the slots themselves are fine.

---

## The five types

### 1. `turn` — the direction changes
The argument stops going one way and goes another. Diagnosis → design.
Assumption → correction. Their model → the real model.

- **Line shape:** *"So the problem isn't X. It's Y — and that changes what we
  should build."*
- **Use at:** the single pivot of the artifact. Once.
- **If missed:** the audience reads the second half as a *different deck*
  stapled to the first, and starts wondering when you will get back to the
  point.

### 2. `narrow` — the aperture closes
From the field to the case. From all the failures to this failure. From the
whole design to the one mechanism that matters.

- **Line shape:** *"Of those four, one is load-bearing. Here it is."*
- **Use at:** every entry into depth. This is the most-used type and the one
  most often left implicit.
- **If missed:** detail arrives feeling arbitrary — the audience cannot tell
  whether the specific thing you are now describing is representative or a
  tangent.

### 3. `escalate` — the stakes rise
The same subject, but it now matters more. Annoyance → cost. Cost → risk. Risk
→ the thing that already happened.

- **Line shape:** *"That's the inconvenient version. Here's the version that
  already cost us something."*
- **Use at:** the problem beats, and never more than twice in a row without a
  `narrow` or a `turn` between — sustained escalation reads as alarmism and
  the audience discounts it.
- **If missed:** the problem stays intellectual. A room that understands a
  problem but does not feel its weight will agree with you and do nothing.

### 4. `fold` — the threads come together
Separate strands, previously handled apart, are now one thing.

- **Line shape:** *"Four different incidents. One data model underneath all
  of them."*
- **Use at:** the end of a multi-part middle, immediately before the close.
  This is the transition that earns the artifact its right to a conclusion.
- **If missed:** the audience leaves holding a list instead of an idea, and a
  list does not survive the walk back to their desk.

### 5. `gate` — a decision is put in the room
The artifact stops presenting and starts asking. Everything before was input;
what follows requires the audience to do something.

- **Line shape:** *"That's the case. The part that needs you is this."*
- **Use at:** exactly one boundary, immediately before `decision`/`ask`. Never
  earlier — a gate opened mid-argument invites the room to decide on partial
  information, and they will.
- **If missed:** the ask lands as a suggestion. The most common ending failure
  is a strong argument that simply stops.

---

## The stakes ladder

Persuasion moves through four states, in order, and each rung has to be
climbed. Skipping one does not save time — it relocates the failure to the
end, where it is expensive.

| Rung | The audience now… | Staged by | If skipped |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Recognised** | sees the situation as theirs, not yours | the orientation beat; a fact from *their* world, not yours | Everything downstream lands as someone else's problem. The room is polite and unmoved. |
| **Understood** | can restate the mechanism in their own words | the mechanism beats, in the order the mechanism runs | They agree it sounds good and cannot defend it to the person who was not in the room — so nothing propagates. |
| **Convinced** | believes it is true, including the parts that cost you | evidence at the point of distrust, plus the named tradeoff | They believe you rather than the argument, and the belief evaporates on contact with the first sceptic. |
| **Moved** | knows the first concrete step and holds it | the ask, with a name and a date | The classic ending: unanimous agreement, no action, and a follow-up meeting to decide what the meeting decided. |

Two ways to use the ladder while staging:

1. **As allocation.** Map every slot to a rung. If eleven of fourteen slots
   sit on *Understood*, the artifact is a manual and the room will not move.
   Recognised and Moved are usually under-slotted.
2. **As diagnosis when the middle sags.** A sag has three signatures and the
   fix is always re-sequencing, never more energy in delivery:
   - **Early sag** — orientation ran too long; the situation beat got more
     than two slots. Cut it to one.
   - **Middle sag** — the tension resolved too early; you answered the killer
     objection before the audience felt it. Move the resolution later, or move
     the escalation earlier.
   - **Late sag** — resolution with no ask; you climbed to *Convinced* and
     stopped. Add the gate.
