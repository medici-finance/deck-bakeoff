# CONSERVATION PLATE

*A visual movement. Written 2026-08-27 as step 1 of the canvas-design process, before
any artifact was drawn. Constrained by the house brand: a true-black canvas, one
Electric Blue accent used no more than twice per field, IBM Plex Sans and IBM Plex Mono
and nothing else.*

---

**Philosophy: what is conserved can be drawn. What is absent must be drawn too.**

Conservation Plate treats every surface as a plate from an atlas of an imaginary
discipline — the kind of engraved page where a phenomenon nobody can hold is
nevertheless measured, ruled, indexed and filed. The plate does not argue. It records.
Its authority comes from the evident labour of the recording: hairlines that meet
exactly, tick fields counted out one mark at a time, numerals set in a monospace that
lets a reader check the columns with a fingertip. The movement's first conviction is
that a quantity which stays constant across a field is the most beautiful thing a
draughtsman can show, and its second is that the most demanding subject is an absence —
a line that is not there, a value that never arrives — because an absence can only be
proved by the surrounding structure being drawn so precisely that its gap becomes
visible. Nothing on a Conservation Plate is sketched. Every mark is placed as though it
will be measured by someone at the top of the field, because it will be.

**Space and form.** The plate is a dark field with a registration frame — an inset
hairline rectangle with corner ticks, the mark of a page that expects to be printed,
cut and archived. Inside it, one figure and one sentence. Never two figures competing;
never a sentence that explains what the figure already shows. The figure occupies the
majority of the field and the sentence sits at its edge like a specimen label. Negative
space is not emptiness but calibrated air: the frame's margins are constant across
every plate in a series so that flipping between them feels like flipping between pages
of one instrument's record book, not between slides someone assembled. Repetition is
the binding: the same rail, the same margin, the same numeral position, plate after
plate, until the eye stops noticing the container and reads only what changed inside it.

**Colour and material.** The ground is true black, and almost everything on it is ink:
white for the value being asserted, graphite for the apparatus, near-invisible grey for
the machinery of measurement. Colour is not decoration and is not distributed — it is
*spent*. One accent, twice per plate at most, marks the single element the eye must
find first: the strike, the live edge, the thing under test. A plate that feels
colourful has failed. The restraint is what makes the two accented marks read as
instrumentation rather than styling, and the discipline of spending them is exactly the
discipline that separates a designed system from a decorated one. The gold of the house
mark appears only inside the mark itself, at the opacity of a watermark pressed into
paper — a maker's stamp, never a highlight.

**Scale and rhythm.** Marks accumulate. A field of two hundred hairline ticks, each
1px, each placed on a grid computed rather than eyeballed, carries more conviction than
any illustration, because the viewer can feel the patience in it. Rhythm comes from
counting: tick, tick, tick, then a longer tick every fifth, then a numeral. Scale comes
from one deliberate rupture of that rhythm — a single mark twice the height of its
neighbours, a single number set large enough to be read across a room. The movement is
quiet almost everywhere so that one loud moment per series lands like a struck bell.
Where the plate moves, motion is measurement: things assemble, accumulate, strain or
stop. Motion that merely pleases is struck from the plate. A gesture that begins and
then refuses to complete is the movement's signature — the strongest way to draw a
thing that does not exist.

**Composition and hierarchy.** Every plate carries its own provenance in small
monospace at the foot: what it is, where its numbers came from, which page of the
record it belongs to. This is not administrative debris; it is the movement's ethic
made visible, and it is set with the same care as the headline. Hierarchy is built from
weight, size and air only — one family for prose, one for data, never a contrasting
face brought in for drama. The largest thing on the plate is the figure; the second
largest is one sentence; the third is a numeral. Everything else is a label, and labels
whisper. Nothing overlaps. Nothing touches the frame. Nothing is clipped. Every element
sits on a common grid and every gap between elements is a value from the same short
scale, checked and re-checked until the composition would survive being enlarged to
poster size and inspected with a rule.

**Craft.** The finished plate must look like the product of many unhurried hours: a
draughtsman's page, not an export. That standard is met in the details nobody is
supposed to notice — the tick field whose last mark lands exactly on the axis end, the
caption whose baseline aligns with the figure's baseline, the curve whose control
points were computed from the same expression the caption names, the numerals that
align because tabular figures were switched on deliberately. Refinement, when it comes,
must subtract: tighten an alignment, quiet a grey, remove a mark that was doing a job
another mark already did. Adding is almost always the wrong instinct. A plate is
finished when nothing else can be taken away without losing a measurement — and when a
stranger, seeing it, would assume it took someone at the absolute top of their field a
very long time.
